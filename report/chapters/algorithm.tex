\section{Algorithms}

In this section, we propose algorithms to efficiently compute the subresultant polynomials of two polynomials $f,g\in R[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ with coefficients in $R=\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, or $\mathbb{F}_p$ with sufficiently large $p$, by using an evaluation-interpolation approach. We recall the results from the previous section:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The subresultants of two evaluated polynomials are equal to the subresultants evaluated at the same point, outside the set of points where either $\lc_{x_1}(f)$ or $\lc_{x_1}(g)$ vanishes.
  \item The degree bound for subresultants is $\deg_{x_1}(f)\deg_{x_2,\dots,x_n}(g) + \deg_{x_1}(g)\deg_{x_2,\dots,x_n}(f)$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Algorithm for bivariate subresultants}

For bivariate polynomials $f,g\in R[x_1,x_2]$, the degree bound of their resultant is given by $\deg_{x_1}(f)\deg_{x_2}(g)+\deg_{x_1}(g)\deg_{x_2}(f)$. Using the above facts, Algorithm \ref{alg:res} computes the subresultant polynomials of $f$ and $g$ by evaluating $f$ and $g$ at sufficiently many points, computing the subresultant polynomials of the evaluated univariate polynomials, and then interpolating to recover the subresultant polynomials of $f$ and $g$. In Algorithm \ref{alg:res}, we denote by $\textsf{Interpolate}(x,y)$ the interpolation method from the set of points $(x_i,y_i)_{1\leq i\leq N+1}$, which is provided by FLINT in our implementation.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
  \caption{\textsf{BivariateSubresultantPolynomials}}\label{alg:res}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $f,g\in R[x_1,x_2]$, $p=\deg_{x_1}(f)$, $q=\deg_{x_1}(g)$, $p>q$
    \Ensure $(\sResP_i(f,g))_{0\leq i\leq q}$
    \State $N \gets \deg_{x_1}(f)\deg_{x_2}(g)+\deg_{x_1}(g)\deg_{x_2}(f)$
    \State // We choose evaluation points starting from $0$ and increasing,
    \State // until we have $N + 1$ points where leading coefficients do not vanish
    \State $\eta \gets 0$
    \For{$i = 1$ to $N + 1$}
    \While{$\lc_{x_1}(f)(\eta) = 0$ or $\lc_{x_1}(g)(\eta) = 0$}
    \State $\eta \gets \eta + 1$
    \EndWhile
    \State $x^i \gets \eta$
    \State $\eta \gets \eta + 1$
    \EndFor
    \State // We compute the subresultant polynomials specialized at the evaluation points
    \For{$i = 1$ to $N + 1$}
    \For{$j = 0$ to $q$}
    \State $y_j^i \gets \sResP_j(f(x^i),g(x^i))$
    \EndFor
    \EndFor
    \State // We interpolate the coefficients to recover the subresultant polynomials
    \For{$j = 0$ to $q$}
    \State $\sResP_j \gets \textsf{Interpolate}(x, y_j)$
    \EndFor
    \State \Return $(\sResP_j)_{0\leq j\leq q}$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Note that interpolation points can also be chosen with another method, e.g. randomly on a given bit size or in the order of increasing bit size. For $R=\mathbb{Z}$, a good choice is to choose points in the order $0, 1, -1, 2, -2, \dots$, etc. We implement these different approaches in our work and compare their performance in the following sections.

\subsection{Generalization to trivariate and multivariate subresultants}

For trivariate polynomials $f,g\in R[x_1,x_2,x_3]$, we can apply the same evaluation-interpolation approach as in the bivariate case, but with a different choice of evaluation points. Specifically, we evaluate at points of the form $(\eta_2,\eta_3)$, chosen so that neither $\lc_{x_1}(f)$ nor $\lc_{x_1}(g)$ vanishes at these points, then perform multivariate interpolation to recover the subresultant polynomials of $f$ and $g$. When fixing $\eta_2$, however, one must also ensure that $\lc_{x_1}(f)$ and $\lc_{x_1}(g)$ are not identically zero as polynomials in $x_3$.

The same idea extends recursively to polynomials in more than three variables, where we evaluate at points of the form $(\eta_2,\dots,\eta_n)$, chosen so that $\lc_{x_1}(f)$ and $\lc_{x_1}(g)$ do not vanish at these points.
